Outreach

We think science is an outward activity. A great deal of research culture points inward, toward other specialists. We would rather go out, and bring people in.

That belief is not decoration on the research. It changes how the lab spends its time. We take the work into schools and community groups, we host visitors in the laboratory, and we build things for people who will never read our papers. Children who visit leave with something they made. Some of them send drawings back.

What we make for people outside the lab

WormTV

A YouTube channel of Caenorhabditis elegans biology, made as an open resource for anyone curious about the animal. It has been watched more than 41,000 times. Visit the channel.

A book for children

We wrote and published a book introducing children to the worm and to the idea of looking closely at living things. We give copies away at outreach events, and it is also available to buy. Find the book.

The visitor art gallery

Children who visit the lab often draw what they saw. We keep those drawings, and we show them, because a drawing of a worm by a seven year old is a record of a scientific observation. See the gallery.

Who we have worked with

College Mentors for Kids. Boy Scouts of America. The Illinois State University Child Care Center. Normal Community High School. We arrange visits for any community member who asks, and we have run after-school sessions designed to build an early appetite for the natural world rather than to teach a syllabus.

What we actually do with visitors

The activities are chosen for the age of the group, and all of them end with the visitor having done something rather than watched something.

Extracting DNA

Students build a model of DNA from pipe cleaners and marshmallows, then extract real DNA from frozen peas, or from their own cheek cells, using household reagents. It takes thirty to forty five minutes, and the point lands when the DNA becomes visible.

Magnetic worms

Children shape a worm from modelling clay, which we bake in the laboratory autoclave for about forty five minutes. A magnet and a pair of googly eyes go on afterwards, and the worm goes home on a fridge door.

Asking a worm what it likes

For fifth and sixth graders. The group first votes on its own favourite condiments, then tests whether real worms move toward or away from each one on an agar plate. Popcorn butter is a reliable worm favourite.

Building an electromagnet

Students wind copper wire around a bolt, make a working electromagnet, and then go around the room testing what is and is not magnetic. Thirty to forty five minutes, and it scales up or down easily.

If you want us to visit, or to visit us

Write to avidal@ilstu.edu. Tell us the age of the group, roughly how many, and how long we have. We will tell you honestly whether what we do will work for them.
