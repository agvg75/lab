Join the lab

Two graduate positions, starting Fall 2027

Duchenne muscular dystrophy. This is the position we are most actively seeking. Human muscle cell culture, organoids and mouse ex vivo physiology. The question is how calcium handling goes wrong in dystrophic muscle, and what mechanical load does to it. It suits someone who wants to work with cells and tissue. It also suits someone willing to build a measurement when none exists.

Magnetic orientation. How a nervous system detects the Earth's magnetic field. We are open to this position for the right person. The emphasis has to be the molecular and physiological mechanism, rather than behaviour alone. Expect genetics, calcium imaging and electrophysiology. Nobody in the lab is currently on this question, so whoever takes it has it to themselves.

One position is a PhD and the other is an MS. If your interests sit near either of these rather than squarely on them, write anyway. Say what you want to work on, and say which degree you are after.

Where the lab's weight sits, plainly

Duchenne muscular dystrophy carries most of the lab's people and most of its funding. Most candidates arrive wanting to work on it, and our recruiting is weighted there. The sensory and magnetic side has produced a comparable share of our publications, with a fraction of the hands. It is thin rather than finished. A crowded, well supported project and a question you would largely have to yourself are both on offer here. They are not the same experience. We would rather you chose with that in front of you.

You will build things

Our questions tend to need instruments that do not exist, or that cost more than we can justify. So the lab builds them. Behavioural assays. A worm tracker costing about thirty dollars. Magnetic field hardware. The RGBCaMP reporter animal. And WINK, the imaging and kinematics platform the lab now runs on. About a fifth of what we publish is a tool, an assay or a method rather than a result. You do not need to arrive knowing how to do any of that. You should find the prospect interesting rather than alarming. The alternative to building the instrument is usually not doing the experiment.

What we are looking for

Previous research experience, by whatever route. And the habit of following a question without being chased. The most useful thing you can tell us in a first email is simple. What was the last thing you did at a bench, or built, that nobody asked you to do?

Both positions are supported. Funding comes from research assistantships on grant funds, and from teaching assistantships through the School of Biological Sciences. We will tell you plainly which you are being offered, and for how long, before you commit to anything.

How to write

Email avidal@ilstu.edu. Say which of the two directions interests you, and why. Say what you have done, and what you want to be able to do by the end. A CV helps. A generic letter addressed to no lab in particular does not.

Formal application is through the School of Biological Sciences at Illinois State University. Writing first is worth more than applying cold.

Undergraduate research

We take undergraduates continuously, and we particularly encourage sophomores and juniors. Students typically give about ten hours a week. They are trained across the whole project rather than handed one technique. That means reading the literature, designing the experiment, running it, analysing it, and presenting it. Most of our undergraduates present posters, and a good number co-author papers. Research hours can be taken for credit through BSC 290 or BSC 299.

Who has come through here

226 people have trained in this lab since 2015. Of the alumni whose destination we know, 89.1 per cent continued in STEM. We know where 81 per cent of them went. One former graduate student takes up a faculty position in 2027. The alumni page lists where people went. That is the question worth asking of any lab you are considering.
