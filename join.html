Join the lab

Two graduate positions, starting Fall 2027

Duchenne muscular dystrophy. This is the position we are most actively seeking. Human muscle cell culture, organoids and mouse ex vivo physiology. The question is how calcium handling goes wrong in dystrophic muscle, and what mechanical load does to it. It suits someone who wants to work with cells and tissue. It also suits someone willing to build a measurement when none exists.

Magnetic orientation. How a nervous system detects the Earth's magnetic field. We are open to this position for the right person. The emphasis has to be the molecular and physiological mechanism, rather than behavior alone. Expect genetics, calcium imaging and electrophysiology. Nobody in the lab is currently on this question, so whoever takes it has it to themselves.

One position is a PhD and the other is an MS. If your interests sit near either of these rather than squarely on them, write anyway. Say what you want to work on, and say which degree you are after.

Where the lab's weight sits, plainly

Duchenne muscular dystrophy carries most of the lab's people and most of its funding. Most candidates arrive wanting to work on it, and our recruiting is weighted there. The sensory and magnetic side has produced a comparable share of our publications, with a fraction of the hands. It is thin rather than finished. A crowded, well supported project and a question you would largely have to yourself are both on offer here. They are not the same experience. We would rather you chose with that in front of you.

You will build things

Our questions tend to need instruments that do not exist, or that cost more than we can justify. So the lab builds them. Behavioral assays. A worm tracker costing about thirty dollars. Magnetic field hardware. The RGBCaMP reporter animal. And WINK, the imaging and kinematics platform the lab now runs on. About a fifth of what we publish is a tool, an assay or a method rather than a result. You do not need to arrive knowing how to do any of that. You should find the prospect interesting rather than alarming. The alternative to building the instrument is usually not doing the experiment.

What we are looking for

Previous research experience, by whatever route. And the habit of following a question without being chased. The most useful thing you can tell us in a first email is simple. What was the last thing you did at a bench, or built, that nobody asked you to do?

Both positions are supported. Funding comes from research assistantships on grant funds, and from teaching assistantships through the School of Biological Sciences. We will tell you plainly which you are being offered, and for how long, before you commit to anything.

How to write

Email avidal@ilstu.edu. Say which of the two directions interests you, and why. Say what you have done, and what you want to be able to do by the end. A CV helps. A generic letter addressed to no lab in particular does not.

Writing first is not optional. The program does not admit students who have not secured a host lab, so the email comes before the application. Deadlines and requirements are below.

Questions people ask, and some they do not

Open whichever apply to you. These are the answers we would give by email anyway.

Do I need experience with C. elegans?

No. The lab runs worms, human muscle cell lines, and from 2027 mouse ex vivo preparations. The Duchenne position is mostly cells and tissue rather than worms. What matters is that you have worked on something at a bench and know what it feels like when an experiment does not cooperate.

What would I actually be doing in the first year?

A defined piece of work that can be finished, alongside coursework and learning the instruments the lab runs on. New people here get a narrow, tractable scope on purpose. A first project that cannot be completed teaches you very little, and an open ended one is the most common way a good student stalls. The scope widens once you have landed one result of your own.

What if I have never built an instrument?

Almost nobody arrives having built one. The worm tracker costs about thirty dollars and is a reasonable first build. What we are looking for is your reaction to the idea. If the prospect of making the thing you need sounds interesting rather than alarming, you will be fine.

I am not sure whether I want an MS or a PhD.

Say so in your first email. We have one of each, so the question is live rather than awkward. An MS here is a complete piece of research with a defense, and it is a working route into a PhD elsewhere. Four of our graduate alumni went on to doctoral programs, including at the University of California San Diego and Loyola University Chicago. Choosing the longer degree because it seems more serious is a poor reason, and we would rather talk about it before you apply than two years in.

Illinois State is not an R1. Does that matter?

It changes some things and not others. The cohort is small, so you work with the PI directly rather than through a layer of postdocs, and you use the instruments instead of queuing for them. We have fewer core facilities than a large research university, which is part of why we build our own.

The output is the part worth checking rather than taking on faith. Ten students have completed a graduate degree in this lab. Graduate students here generate a median of fifteen outputs, counting posters, conference talks and peer reviewed papers. A master's student typically leaves with around thirteen presentations and one or two papers. A doctoral student leaves with around twenty seven presentations and four papers. That runs at three to four outputs per year in the lab. Every one of the ten is an author on at least one peer reviewed paper and at least one poster. Preprints and software releases are counted separately, and several students have those as well.

We treat that record as the point rather than a by-product. An output is the durable, externally checkable evidence that you finished something, and which outputs matter depends on where you are going. For research positions it is papers and presentations. For teaching it is the mentoring and instruction you have actually done, which is why our students mentor undergraduates and high school researchers rather than only being mentored. We count conference talks as well as posters, because giving a talk means a student was far enough along to stand up and hold a room, which is a different skill from producing a result. The publication list and the posters and talks are both public, so you can count for yourself. Not everyone who begins a graduate degree finishes one, here or anywhere, so those are the people who completed rather than everyone who passed through.

As for where people go, a former undergraduate from this lab took a PhD at Harvard and is a postdoctoral fellow there, one graduate alumna is a postdoctoral fellow at Scripps Research, and another takes up an assistant professorship in 2027. Across all alumni whose destination we know, 89.1 percent continued in STEM.

Per student and per dollar, we think that record stands comparison. The alumni page is there so you can check it rather than take our word for it.

Who pays me, and for how long?

Every admitted student is supported, through a teaching assistantship, a research assistantship, or both, for as long as they continue to meet the program's requirements. Research assistantships come from grant funds and teaching assistantships through the School of Biological Sciences. Most students see both over the course of a degree. We will tell you which you are being offered, and for how long, before you commit to anything.

How do I actually apply, and when?

Write to me first. That is a prerequisite rather than a courtesy. The program does not admit a student without a faculty member willing to host them, so the email has to happen before the application is worth submitting. The application itself goes to the School of Biological Sciences, and the admission decision belongs to the program rather than to me. What I control is whether there is a place in this lab.

The deadline for a fall start is 1 February, so a Fall 2027 start means 1 February 2027. You will need transcripts, a CV, a two page statement of goals and three letters. The GRE is optional and carries little weight. The stated minimum is a 3.0 grade point average over your last sixty undergraduate hours, and the doctoral program expects considerable prior research experience, whether from a master's, undergraduate research or elsewhere.

International applicants: the department has a large international contingent, so this is routine here rather than exceptional, and the support described above applies in the same way. You will also submit an English proficiency score unless you qualify for a waiver. Requirements change, so check the program pages for the current version: MS in Biological Sciences and PhD in Biological Sciences.

Undergraduate research

We take undergraduates continuously, and we particularly encourage sophomores and juniors. Students typically give about ten hours a week. They are trained across the whole project rather than handed one technique. That means reading the literature, designing the experiment, running it, analyzing it, and presenting it. Most of our undergraduates present posters, and a good number co-author papers. Research hours can be taken for credit through BSC 290 or BSC 299.
