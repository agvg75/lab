How this lab uses AI

We run a good deal of this laboratory on AI systems. Here is what they do, what they are not allowed to do, and what has gone wrong.

Why we publish this

People increasingly ask how we use these tools, and the honest answer is involved enough that it deserves a page rather than a sentence. We also think the method by which a laboratory organizes its science is part of the science. If we are willing to publish our mentoring outcomes alongside the national comparison data that makes them look less flattering, we should be willing to describe how the work gets organized, including the parts that have failed.

Nothing here is a product endorsement and none of it is unusual technology. What follows is a description of practice.

A research program is a lattice, and the lattice is the hard part

This lab currently holds thirteen people, three graduate students, nine undergraduates and one high school student, and each owes a degree or a credential with its own milestones. A dozen manuscripts sit at different stages, each needing particular data from particular hands. Several funding mechanisms are in play across years, and each is triggered by a specific result rather than by a date. Three model systems run at once, worms, human cells and mouse tissue, and the scientific argument depends on them agreeing.

The lattice only pays off when the pieces line up. A student's thesis chapter should also be a manuscript, which should also be the preliminary data for the grant that funds the next student, who takes over the assay when the first one graduates. Getting that right across five years and everyone who passes through in that time is the actual work of running a laboratory. Written into a spreadsheet, it becomes a spreadsheet nobody opens. Held in one person's head, it survives exactly until something changes without warning.

Things change without warning. A technique walks out of the door with the person who owned it. A laser quietly loses a tenfold on one excitation path between two sessions. A paper is rejected. A supplier cannot provide the material a plan depended on. In every case the question that matters is not what broke, but what else now has to move.

That is what these systems are for.

What they do

Designing the program across people, papers and years. Which question gets asked, by whom, toward what degree, landing in which manuscript, becoming preliminary data for which proposal, and by when. The reasoning is not difficult; the bookkeeping is. The number of dependencies among that many people, a dozen papers and several funding mechanisms over five years is larger than anyone tracks reliably, and a missed one is not noticed for months. The value is clearest under disruption, when a single departure has to be traced through scopes, manuscripts, preliminary data, training windows and recruiting in one sitting rather than in pieces over a semester.

Grants, across mechanisms and across years. Every proposal this lab has submitted generated reviewer critiques. Individually they are read once, painfully, and filed. Read together across mechanisms and across years, they say something no single review can: what reviewers consistently credit, what they consistently object to, and which arguments have survived. The most useful result so far was a validation rather than a correction. Pairing worms with human cells and mouse tissue costs real effort and a reviewer could fairly call it unfocused; the accumulated reviews show it is what reviewers credit. That came out of documents we already had and had never read collectively.

Manuscripts, as an in-house reviewer number two. Before a manuscript leaves, something that did not produce the work goes through the controls, the design and the calculations looking for what a hostile reviewer would find. It has caught a comparison across two cohorts that were not comparable, corrected how shared control arms should be analyzed, and forced a causal claim down to the weaker one the data actually support. It is also wrong often enough to be instructive: in one audit, five of its initial findings were overturned once the source workbooks were opened, which is why we now check primary sources before reporting an error.

Mentoring and trainee development. Mentorship plans, expectations, and intake and outtake questionnaires that the students they concern can actually open and that get updated, rather than signed once and filed. Honest position reporting, because when a student's timeline is not supported by their output, a record showing them where they stand does more than another conversation. And checking the real admissions and support requirements against the institution's current pages when someone writes asking to join, rather than answering from memory.

Mining what we already have. The lab's archive holds years of recordings and measurements collected for questions that have since been answered. Characterizing what is actually in there, what condition it is in, and which questions it could still answer is work nobody had the hours to do by hand. Data already collected is data already paid for, and most laboratories never go back to it.

Tools, and strategies our people can use without me in the room. We build the instruments we cannot buy, and we assemble analysis strategies, some taken from the field and some developed here, so that a student designing an experiment or working through a result has somewhere to start that is not a conversation with the principal investigator. Expertise that only exists in one person's head is a bottleneck and a single point of failure.

Obligations and scheduling. Research, teaching, service, committees and travel, in one place, which is harder than it sounds mostly for unglamorous reasons: our institutional mail and calendar system cannot be connected to any of this, so a forwarding rule carries part of it and the rest is read and summarized each morning.

The system bends to the people, not the other way round

Every system that depends on more than one person dies the same way. It asks people to change how they work, they comply for three weeks, and then it quietly becomes a thing nobody opens. We have done this to ourselves more than once, which is why it now has a design rule of its own.

The rule is an inversion. A student records their data in whatever way suits them and suits the experiment in front of them, and the burden of translating it into the form the laboratory needs falls on the system rather than on the student. The adjustment happens on our side. This is slower to build and it is the only version that survives contact with a working lab, because the alternative asks a twenty year old at a microscope at eleven at night to serve the convenience of a database.

There is a second reason to let people work in their own way, and it caught us by surprise. A student learning to film worms produces uneven footage: inconsistent lighting, drifting focus, variable framing. For measuring anything that is a problem. For a model learning to cope with real input rather than ideal input it is close to ideal material. The variability we would once have written off as beginner's work is part of what makes the resulting tools robust, which puts a trainee's learning curve on the asset side of the ledger rather than the cost side.

What they are not allowed to do

They act on their own only where the action is reversible. Such actions are labelled provisional until a named person confirms or withdraws them. Filed results, sealed data, and anything that leaves the laboratory are decided by a person, and the record says which person. Saying there is a human in the loop means nothing unless you can say which human, at which point, with the standing to overrule what.

A line older than seven days is a claim, not a fact. Every session is instructed to read the record that way. That single sentence has prevented more errors here than any technical measure we have built.

Every decision records what would overturn it. When we settle a question we write down the reasoning and the evidence that would change our minds. It is the only way to tell later between a conclusion that still holds and one whose conditions quietly expired.

A perimeter governs what leaves the laboratory, and the line is whether an unmade claim rides on the data. Data we have already published can go to the shared system, because the claim has been made. So can data that was never going to be published. What stays in place, on machines we control, is everything in flight: manuscripts in preparation, tools under development, and the results heading into them. The automated analysis system supported by our Anthropic award operates under an agreement that its work is shared with them, so it sees only the first two categories.

Data rejected for one purpose is not useless data. A recording too coarse to resolve pharyngeal pumping may be entirely adequate to train a locomotion detector. Fitness for purpose belongs to the measurement rather than to the file, so footage that failed the experiment it was shot for frequently has a second life. A deliberate fraction of what we collect is acquired for that use and no other, not published by design, with no result waiting to be reported from it.

Methods cross in both directions. Models we develop and approaches that turn out to work move freely between the two sides. That exchange is the point of the arrangement.

Trainee records stay inside the perimeter. Nothing identifying a student is contributed to model training or to any shared programme, and several categories of record never leave their own compartment at all.

No statistic reaches a manuscript or a grant on their word alone. It must be corroborated by a system that did not produce it and by a human. Ours is a co-investigator and statistician colleague, who has verified the statistics on a manuscript under this rule.

They do not treat me as the source of truth. It is written into the system that no claim is believed without a source, mine included, and that what I say is provisional until independently checked. One of my own figure miscounts was caught this way.

A clean result has to say how much it read. A check that silently read nothing and reported success has happened five times here. It is the most common way these systems mislead you.

Acceptance criteria are fixed before the data arrives. For a human versus machine comparison now underway, the criteria were signed and the machine output hashed before the first human scoring session.

One clarification, because it is the sort of thing worth stating before someone asks. Like any laboratory using commercial software, our work passes through the providers whose tools we use, under commercial terms that exclude training on it. We are not claiming that no vendor server ever sees a file. The commitment above is narrower and more specific than that, and it is the one we can actually keep: anything carrying a result we have not yet reported, and anything identifying a trainee, is not contributed to the funded programme and is not used to train anything.

What has gone wrong

A description like this is worth very little if it only lists successes. These are real and recent.

We recorded 468 decisions in nine weeks. Fifty one were ever cited again.

A retrieval rate of eleven percent, counted on 6 November 2026. We solved the problem of writing things down and did not notice for a long time that we had not solved the problem of finding them later.

Our master tracker went 57 days without being opened while still looking authoritative.

Its design was sound and its formulas were correct. A program reading it received stale numbers with no error and no warning. A stale record that still looks current is more dangerous than no record, because it is believed.

An obligations display went live showing seven of sixty two records.

The software was not at fault. Only dated entries could reach a view, and the feed being written carried no dates. One item was listed as outstanding while the notice of its completion sat in the inbox. It recurred the same week in a new form, and the fix was to make the daily triage hunt for evidence that something had been resolved as well as for new things to do.

A rule nobody checks is a rule that has stopped.

A daily rebuild of our own state file was missed for nine days. The rule was written down, and so was a tripwire for exactly that failure, and neither was being checked by anything that runs. In the same week eight hours of scheduled overnight work did not happen because a login had lapsed, silently. Writing a lesson down is not the same as enforcing it: in one day we repeated twenty seven mistakes across nine patterns, eight of which were already recorded in our own lessons file. Every scheduled thing needs an alarm for its own silence.

A measurement bar was written around a quantity nobody had defined.

Tolerances were set for a human versus machine comparison without first asking what the scored object was. They turned out to be about one percent of the real counts, and one could not have been met by the reference compared against itself. The real question, which no one had asked, was whether the human and the machine were naming the same objects at all.

A specification sat unreadable for five days.

Three files were uploaded to shared storage and two arrived corrupt, including one a developer was waiting on. Nobody noticed because the file size looked plausible. The rule requiring every write to be read back already existed and was not followed.

The last two happened in the same week this page was written. The system did not prevent them. What it did was make them findable, attributable and fixable within a day, which is the honest claim available and the only one worth making.

The claim we would defend

Not that this laboratory is efficient, and not that any of this is solved. The narrower claim is that a small group can keep a multi-year program coherent across people who arrive, learn something nobody else knows, and leave, and across manuscripts, model systems and funding mechanisms that each depend on the others, while parts of it break without warning.

Doing that needs a written record the next session can find, with identifiers, dates and a stated perimeter, with the discipline of quoting people verbatim and labelling everything else by how it was adopted, and with acceptance criteria fixed before the data arrives. The AI systems are how that record gets written, read and reasoned over at a volume a person could not sustain. They are not the thing itself.
