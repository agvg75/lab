How living matter measures the physical world, and acts on it

An animal is not separate from the universe. It is the universe, doing something. A pattern that keeps itself going, the way a wave is water in motion. To persist, that pattern has to exchange matter and energy. It samples what carries information bearing on its own survival. It integrates that with what it is already doing. It selects an action, and then carries it out. We call that process behavior. We study the whole loop, and what happens when it is disrupted.

That is one question, not four. The lab looks like several projects from outside. It looks that way because the loop has stages, and each stage needs its own instrument. A worm orienting in a magnetic field, a worm deciding whether it sits in water or in soil, and a dystrophic muscle failing under load are the same problem. They sit at different points on the same circuit. The matter being sensed is sometimes called the outside world and sometimes the animal itself. That split is bookkeeping done by the observer. The animal is measuring one continuous physical system that it happens to be part of.

What we are looking for and how to apply

The loop, and where our work sits on it

Sampling: what information is even available

Some of what an animal needs to know is barely available at all. The Earth's magnetic field is extremely weak. We showed that C. elegans orients to it, and that the AFD sensory neurons are required. Temperature, humidity and the mechanics of the surroundings are others. Humidity has no receptor of its own. The animal infers it by combining mechanical and thermal input. Our open question here is the hardest one in the lab. How does a field that weak become a signal a neuron can read?

Processing: turning signals into a decision

Signals have to be weighed before they are useful. They are not weighed by neurons alone. Work in revision shows that the AMsh glia set the duration of a touch induced escape response. That puts a non-neuronal cell inside a sensory decision. Earlier work showed that dopamine and serotonin select between crawling and swimming. The decision is chemical as much as it is circuit level.

Executing: producing force, and knowing that you did

Here what is being measured is the animal. Not instead of the universe. The animal is part of it, so proprioception and magnetoreception are one problem, and the word we use depends only on where the measurement lands. The mechanoreceptor pezo-1 is required for normal crawling. Its isoforms do different jobs. Its expression in muscle itself contributes differently to swimming and to crawling. That result is about proprioception rather than strength. Muscle is not only the actuator. It is also a sensor, reporting on its own state.

Responding to disruption

Nothing in the loop is guaranteed to keep working. We break it deliberately, in three ways. From inside the genome, Duchenne muscular dystrophy damages the actuator. Because muscle is also a sensor, it corrupts what the animal knows about its own body. That is a lesion in two places on the loop at once. We put load on an animal using burrowing, a behavior that demands far more force than crawling. Dystrophic muscle declines faster under it. That result appeared in PNAS in 2019. It bears on how much exercise is advisable for patients.

From the physical environment, we raise animals for six generations under Mars analog gravity and hypomagnetic fields. That removes two physical constants nothing on Earth developed without. From the biotic environment, we independently validated the transgenerational inheritance of learned pathogen avoidance. Information about a threat outlives the animal that met it.

Most of the lab works on the first of the three. All three ask one question. Can a living system keep going when its assumptions stop holding?

Building what does not exist

These questions need instruments that do not exist, or that cost more than we can justify. So we build them, and we count that as research. The list is long. The swimming and crawling comparison. A family of burrowing assays that put measured load on an animal. A long run of magnetic assays, and the pulser that drives them. A worm tracker costing about thirty dollars. RGBCaMP, a reporter animal that reads calcium in several tissue compartments at once. And WINK (Worm Imaging and Kinematics), our modular analysis platform. WINK follows body shape, the traveling bending wave and calcium per body segment together. The stages of the loop can then be watched in one animal at one time. About a fifth of what the lab publishes is a tool, an assay or a method rather than a result. This work is supported by an Anthropic AI for Science award.

Science is an outward activity

A great deal of research culture points inward. We would rather go out, and bring people in. We take the work into schools and community groups, host visitors in the laboratory, run a C. elegans channel watched more than 41,000 times, and publish a book for children. Young visitors often send us drawings of what they saw, and we keep them. What we do, and how to arrange a visit.

Graduate students, undergraduates and high school researchers in this lab are trained in the whole arc of a project, from reading the literature to presenting results, and most author posters or papers. Our publication list marks every student author, so you can see for yourself how often that happens.
